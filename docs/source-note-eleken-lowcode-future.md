## Why I Am Reading This

This represents the mainstream view I'm pushing back against. The "low-code is the future" narrative is everywhere - I need to understand what claims are being made so I can address them fairly. This source cites Gartner and Forrester projections that get repeated across the industry.

## What This Source Claims

- Gartner: 20% growth in no-code/low-code market, currently ~$14 billion
- Developer shortage: 40 million globally (2021), projected 85.2 million by 2030
- Forrester: 83% value flexibility, 63% value speed, 67% appreciate process automation, 80% say low-code frees developer time
- Mendix: 71% of low-code developers deliver apps in under 3 months
- Low-code "may not completely replace coding, but it could significantly reduce reliance on traditional coding"
- "Critical systems and essential routines" still require custom development

## What It Supports

This helps my essay because:

- Even this pro-low-code source admits limitations: "drag-and-drop approach may fall short in meeting detailed requirements"
- Acknowledges eliminating coding sacrifices "testing, debug, version control and sandboxing"
- Admits "critical systems" still need custom development - my workflow was critical to my business
- The 71% "under 3 months" stat is about *delivery*, not whether it *works well at scale*
- The benefits cited (speed, flexibility, freeing developer time) are about creation, not execution efficiency

## What It Complicates

This challenges or narrows my view because:

- The developer shortage is real (40-85 million). Not everyone can hire a developer, even if they wanted to. That's why I can only serve a few clients at a time. But the economics work for me because my clients run on the same stack - I fractionalize the IT cost across them. That's the model.
- 83% valuing "flexibility" - they're talking about flexibility to create workflows themselves. That's the tinkerers. I'm not talking to tinkerers. I'm talking to people who want to work on their sales process or their product/delivery, not their tools. But also: flexibility is subjective. Is the business owner who emails their in-house dev and gets a custom solution the same week *less flexible* than the business owner who can DIY a workflow? That's not really a valid comparison. From my perspective, I have *more* flexibility when I control the DB, the CRM, the docs, the whole stack. I work faster and can implement more complicated integrations. More flexibility on the end-user's side means more constraints baked into the platform for me to work around.
- The market is $14 billion and growing 20%. But I think this is explained because it's the fast food of software - available to the masses for cheap. Weekend entrepreneurs can now start businesses on these tools, it's all they've ever known. The prices spike fast as you succeed. My guess: the revenue comes from the graveyard of many startups (who paid before failing) plus the few survivors seeing costs balloon as they scale. If all you can afford is no-code, I'm glad it's there for you - have a go. But I don't think the industry revenue growth is all that meaningful as a signal of quality or sustainability.
- My argument applies most to people who *could* access a developer but don't realize the trade-off. For those who genuinely can't, low-code might still be the best option.

## My Actual Audience (Clarification)

My audience is NOT the low-code wizards who've mastered these tools. Those people are coders in disguise - they just don't know it. They spent weekends learning how Zapier works. They debugged broken workflows. They learned to think in systems. Good for them, but they're not who I'm talking to.

My audience is the people who hear those wizards say "this is the future, anyone can do it" and feel like they're failing because it's not simple for them. I'm saying: it's not simple. It never was. The wizards are discounting their own learning curve.

Low-code is just one step removed from coding with more abstractions. I as the expert find it *harder* to work in that environment - the visual interfaces add friction, the platforms have arbitrary constraints. I can only imagine what it feels like for my ICP trying to learn these tools while running a business.

If you're confused and struggling with low-code, that's OK. That's normal. You're not stupid.

**The Scratch comparison:** This reminds me of Scratch - MIT's tool for teaching kids to code. Block-based, visual, drag-and-drop. Scratch is explicitly designed to teach programming concepts without syntax. Workflows are the same thing, just rebranded for business. Nobody claims Scratch isn't programming - they call it "visual programming." But somehow when we rebrand it for adults and call it "no-code," we pretend it's fundamentally different.

## Reliability / Limits

Why I trust or distrust this source:

- Eleken is a design agency that *works with* low-code platforms - they have skin in the game
- They cite Gartner, Forrester, Mendix - but these are all industry sources with their own incentives
- The article is marketing content (ends with a pitch for their design services)
- Still useful because it represents what the industry *believes* about itself

What this source does **not** prove:

- That low-code actually delivers on these promises at scale
- That the 71% "fast delivery" translates to long-term success
- What happens when you hit the customization ceiling
- Any cost comparison between low-code credits vs. custom development
- Whether the "freed developer time" is real or whether devs spend that time debugging low-code limitations
